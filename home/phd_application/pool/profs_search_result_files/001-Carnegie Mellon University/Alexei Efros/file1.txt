# Andrew Carnegie: Main Text Content

Andrew Carnegie (1835-1919) was a Scottish-born American industrialist and philanthropist who became one of the wealthiest individuals of his era through steel manufacturing.

## Early Life

Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland, to William Carnegie, a weaver, and Margaret Morrison. His formal education began at age eight. When mechanization made hand-weaving unprofitable, his family emigrated to the United States in 1848, settling in Allegheny, Pennsylvania.

## Early Career

At thirteen, Carnegie worked as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill earning $1.20 weekly while attending evening classes. By age fourteen, he joined a telegraph office in Pittsburgh, where his abilities attracted Thomas A. Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad. Scott made Carnegie his secretary, and Carnegie advanced to become Pittsburgh division leader. During the Civil War, Carnegie worked with Scott in Washington, D.C., organizing military telegraph systems.

## Steel Industry Success

After the war, Carnegie invested strategically in iron mills and the Keystone Bridge Company. Witnessing Henry Bessemer's steel conversion technology in Britain, he recognized steel's superior potential. In 1870, he constructed his first blast furnace. By 1873, he built the Edgar Thomson Steel Works near Pittsburgh.

Carnegie merged his company with Henry Clay Frick's coke production business in 1881, achieving vertical integration. In 1892, Frick consolidated operations into Carnegie Steel Company, then the world's largest steel producer. Labor tensions erupted at the Homestead plant when Frick cut wages and imported strikebreakers, resulting in deaths and injuries. Carnegie bore public responsibility despite disagreeing with Frick's methods privately.

In 1901, Carnegie sold his enterprise to J.P. Morgan for $480 million, who combined it with others to form U.S. Steel.

## Philanthropic Work

Carnegie famously declared: "The man who dies rich dies in disgrace." He donated over $350 million (approximately $11.9 billion in modern currency) to various causes:

**Libraries**: Between 1889-1923, Carnegie funded 1,681 public libraries in America, donating over $56 million total to construct 2,509 libraries internationally.

**Foundations**: He established the Carnegie Hero Fund Commission (1904) to honor selfless individuals. In 1910, he funded the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace with $10 million. He also co-founded the American Anti-Imperialist League in 1898.

**Education**: Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University) received $10 million in 1900. The Carnegie Institution of Washington received another $10 million in 1902.

**Arts**: Carnegie Hall opened in 1890, financed by Carnegie after meeting conductor Walter Damrosch. He also established the Carnegie International art exhibition.

**Other**: He donated $1.5 million toward The Hague's Peace Palace. In 1911, he founded Carnegie Corporation of New York with $125 million in startup capital, which has distributed $1.35 billion since its establishment.

## Death and Legacy

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts, and was buried at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in Tarrytown, New York. His wife Louise survived him by 26 years. Their only child was daughter Margaret.

Honors include a dinosaur species named after him (Diplodocus carnegii), the Carnegie Medal for literature, and the Carnegie Range in Antarctica.

## Criticism

Biographer Peter Krass noted that Carnegie's selective memory omitted America's labor struggles, slavery, Native American displacement, and women's suffrage issues. While he amassed wealth through steel mills where workers suffered dangerous conditions and low wages, he showed limited empathy for those who built his fortune.
