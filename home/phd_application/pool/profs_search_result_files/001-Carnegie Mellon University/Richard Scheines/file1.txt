Andrew Carnegie - Main Content

Early Life and Background

Andrew Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland, to weaver William Carnegie and Margaret Morrison. At age thirteen, he began working as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill earning $1.20 weekly while attending evening school. By fourteen, he moved to Pittsburgh to work as a telegraph operator.

Career Development

Carnegie's talents caught the attention of Thomas A. Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad, who made him his secretary. During the Civil War, Carnegie worked in Washington organizing military telegraph systems. After the war, he inherited Scott's railroad division but left in 1865 to establish his own businesses.

Recognizing the future of steel production after observing Henry Bessemer's innovations in Britain, Carnegie built his first blast furnace in 1870. His 1873 steel mill, named after Pennsylvania Railroad president J. Edgar Thompson, soon secured major contracts. Through shrewd mergers, particularly with Henry Clay Frick's coke operations in 1881, Carnegie built the world's largest steel company by 1892.

Business Success and Controversy

In 1901, Carnegie sold his company to banker J.P. Morgan for $480 million, making him extraordinarily wealthy. However, his legacy includes the brutal 1892 Homestead strike, where workers protesting wage cuts clashed with strikebreakers, resulting in deaths and injuries.

Philanthropic Legacy

Carnegie declared "The man who dies rich dies in disgrace" and became America's most prominent philanthropist, donating over $350 million (approximately $11.9 billion today). His contributions included:

- Libraries: Funded 2,509 public libraries across America and Europe
- Education: Founded what became Carnegie Mellon University; established the Carnegie Institution of Washington
- Culture: Financed Carnegie Hall in New York
- Peace: Created the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace with $10 million
- Heroism: Established Carnegie Hero Trust Funds recognizing selfless acts

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts, having shaped American culture and institutional life significantly.
