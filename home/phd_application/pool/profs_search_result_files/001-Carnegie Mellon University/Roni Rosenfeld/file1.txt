Andrew Carnegie - Main Content Summary

Basic Information:
Andrew Carnegie (1835-1919) was a Scottish-born American steel tycoon and one of the wealthiest individuals of his era. Born in Dunfermline, Scotland, he immigrated to the United States as a young man and built a vast steel empire before becoming renowned for his philanthropic endeavors.

Business Career:
Carnegie began working at age 13 in textile mills, later transitioning to railroad work. He recognized the potential of steel production after observing industrial advances in Britain. By 1873, he established a steel mill that became the foundation of his industrial empire. He consolidated his operations and eventually sold his company to J.P. Morgan in 1901 for $480 million, which led to the creation of U.S. Steel.

Philanthropic Legacy:
Carnegie became famous for his charitable giving, donating over $350 million during his lifetime. His notable contributions included:

- Libraries: "He wanted to enable access to free public libraries," supporting the construction of 2,509 libraries worldwide
- Carnegie Hall: A world-renowned concert venue he funded in New York
- Educational Institutions: Carnegie Mellon University and the Carnegie Institution for Science
- Peace Initiatives: The Carnegie Endowment for International Peace and contributions to the Peace Palace in The Hague

Philosophy:
In his essay, Carnegie stated: "The man who dies rich dies in disgrace," reflecting his belief that wealth should benefit society.

Criticism:
Despite his philanthropy, Carnegie faced criticism for labor practices, including the controversial Homestead Strike of 1892, where workers died during a violent confrontation.
