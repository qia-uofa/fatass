# Thesis Writer Profile

## Who they are

The writer is a Computer Science bachelor's student at Goethe University Frankfurt, working under supervisor Visvanathan Ramesh. Their officially issued thesis title is **"An Ontological Perspective on LLM Beliefs"** (Themenausgabe: start 27.06.2026, Abgabetermin 31.08.2026 — the same date as this conversation) — note this supersedes the working title "...LLM Confidence" used throughout most of the brainstorming process; the writer caught the mismatch late and it needs to propagate to the title page and any remaining doc text.

They sit at the intersection of mechanistic interpretability and philosophy of language/ontology, which is an unusual and fairly ambitious combination for a bachelor's thesis. The technical core is a replication and extension of Kumaran et al. 2026, "How do LLMs Compute Verbal Confidence?" (Google DeepMind, ICML 2026) — the finding that LLMs cache confidence-related information at answer-adjacent token positions, retrieved later when verbalizing a self-reported confidence level, and that this cached signal explains more variance than raw log-probabilities alone. The philosophical anchor is William Benzon's work on ontology and knowledge representation in LLMs ("Ontology in Knowledge Representation," "ChatGPT's Ontological Landscape"), which supplies the vocabulary (paradigmatic vs. ontological transitions) the writer uses to argue that epistemic emotions (surprise, confusion, doubt) are the affective signature of exactly the categorical-transition moments Benzon describes.

The writer has their own prior, self-run experiments (documented informally in `papers/presentation.md`) predating and feeding into this thesis: a "give me 20 things" prompt-repetition/collapse experiment, and a "20 Questions" experiment testing (and eventually falsifying, after controlling for a confound) a hypothesis about abstract/concrete word frequency bias in ChatGPT's questioning strategy. This shows a writer who already does small, scrappy empirical probing on their own initiative, and who is willing to report a null/negative result rather than discard it.

## Priorities for the thesis

1. **The extension must do real argumentative work, not be a robustness check.** The writer has internalized (from pushback they explicitly asked for and accepted) that the value of this thesis hinges on whether the epistemic-emotion extension can discriminate between ontological readings of confidence — not on replication fidelity alone.
2. **Keep the ontological claim modest.** The realistic, thesis-supportable conclusion is something like "the pattern is more consistent with an information-caching/retrieval architecture than a naive introspective self-report framing" — not a resolved metaphysical claim about what confidence or belief "is."
3. **Title fidelity.** The bound thesis must use the officially issued title, "An Ontological Perspective on LLM Beliefs" — verbatim, per the Themenausgabe letter and §35 topic-issuance rules. Any surviving "...LLM Confidence" language in supporting docs is a stale artifact of the working title, not a deliberate choice.
4. **Honest, disclosed AI use.** Any AI-assisted content (code via `fatass`/Claude, brainstorming/planning) needs to be distinguishable from the writer's own RQs, interpretation, and argument, with a disclosure note and a willingness to point to git/`./log` history as available on request.
5. **Grounding in real, independently checked ground truth.** They pushed hard to understand exactly why confidence has an independent correctness signal (TriviaQA gold answers) and what would be needed for other epistemic states — this rigor about what counts as genuine evidence (vs. circular self-report) should carry through to how results are written up.
