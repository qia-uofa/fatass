William Benzon's working paper "ChatGPT's Ontological Landscape" (November 2023) draws conclusions about how ChatGPT organizes concepts purely by prompting it and reading what comes back. Repetition across trials, he argues, turns up a landscape of ontological categories, a narrative template that keeps recurring across separate runs, and a stable asymmetry between how the model handles concrete versus abstract questions. He reaches these conclusions without touching a weight or an activation: the method is entirely behavioral, prompting the model and judging what the resulting response implies, with nothing else to go on. Nobody doubts that the structure Benzon keeps finding is interesting. The harder question is whether it is a genuine feature of the model or an artifact of how loosely each experiment samples its targets: small, hand-picked trials standing in for a general claim about how ChatGPT organizes concepts. This essay uses Benzon's paper as the case for testing that question, and the sharpest evidence on either side turns out to come from tightening one of his own experiments rather than from anything external.

"ChatGPT's Ontological Landscape" is a paper about method as much as about ChatGPT itself. Benzon prompts the model with minimal, unconstrained instructions and reads the resulting pattern as diagnostic of its internal landscape. Two experiments anchor this. Asked repeatedly for "a list of 20 things, anything," the lists differ item by item but are drawn, in his own words, from "roughly the same conceptual territory": physical objects skewed toward a narrow, recurring set, bicycle, hot air balloon, chocolate chip cookies, rainbow. He reads this through Waddington's epigenetic landscape: the prompt drops a ball into activation space, and it rolls downhill into a valley shaped by the lay of the land. In "ChatGPT Plays 20 Questions," six targets played twice each, bicycle, squid, apple against justice, evolution, truth, gave the concrete trio 19–31 questions with several hints against the abstract pair's 7–9 with none, while truth sat between at 19–21 questions but needed the most hints of any round. The real wager is that repeated, informal trials, without touching weights or activations, can license a claim about category structure, a wager Benzon hedges himself, calling it "premature to ascribe any statistical significance to these findings" and asking "What universe are we sampling and how do we choose our samples?" That hedge is the fault line this essay returns to.

I ran a deliberate variant on Benzon's metaphor myself. I prompted an LLM with "give me 20 things, anything, start with butterfly, dragonfly, ..." The model continued the "-fly" pattern, then, once exhausted, drifted outward through flying bugs, general bugs, and flying mammals; asked for "50 more," it collapsed further into general mammals. Where Benzon's experiment drops the ball into a shallow, broad valley, "things," and watches where it lands, mine does the inverse: it drops the ball into a narrow, seeded pocket and traces its escape back toward the broad valley, testing a mechanism Benzon's metaphor implies but never runs, since his own trials are always fresh twenty-item prompts, not an in-context extension of an existing list. Measured against Benzon's own standard, my experiment is underdeveloped. It records no model version or session date; Benzon, by contrast, logs "the September 25 version of ChatGPT" and the time of each trial. It also runs only once, well short of the three reruns, one after logging out, that Benzon uses to check whether a list is rigid. Temperature goes unaddressed entirely, even though Benzon flags it directly: "A temperature of zero (0) seems to eliminate ALL choice... I would imagine that [the range of choices] varies with the value of the temperature parameter," a variable that would need fixing before a drift claim like this can be trusted.

My second experiment interrogates the subject paper's headline finding most directly. It runs a full hypothesize, predict, test, catch-a-confound, fix, result arc on Benzon's concrete/abstract asymmetry. The hypothesis: ChatGPT's first question is always abstract versus concrete, and because abstract words are rarer in the dictionary, disambiguating an abstract target takes fewer steps, generalized to the claim that a binary question always favors the less-frequent branch first. Then I catch my own confound: the concrete/abstract split among Benzon's six targets does not match the true ratio in the dictionary, raising the possibility that the asymmetry reflects item selection alone, independent of how the model actually questions. The fix samples from a custom dictionary with an equal split and tells ChatGPT the target is drawn from it; the result shows no significant bias toward either category. A second overclaim gets caught along the way: the premise that the first question is "always" abstract or concrete does not hold against Benzon's own transcripts, where opening questions are almost always about animacy or tangibility first, for justice: "Is it a living thing? No / Is it an object? No / Is it a concept or idea? Yes," question three, not one. This is me correcting Benzon's own sampling on his own terms, with the scrutiny his hedge called for but did not perform.

Two other students' presentations function here strictly as stress tests of the landscape/attractor claim already on the table, a role distinct from offering parallel summaries of it. [Student A], presenting on Benzon's later paper "ChatGPT tells 20 versions of its prototypical story," replicated his single-session-versus-separate-session design on open-weight models. Single-session runs were 10/10 coherent, while separate-session runs on Llama-3.1-8B were only 7/10, three degenerating into token salad. [Student A]'s reframe, that session context is what governs stability, with results themselves left largely unaffected, marks the hard-collapse endpoint of the spectrum my own "50 more" experiment traces softly, as genericization rather than incoherence, suggesting degeneration under sustained generation is a spectrum, not a binary. The same presentation's comparison of "story" (7/10) against "tell me a story" and "write a story" (10/10 each) points to prompt specificity as what buys coherence, with session history playing a smaller role than assumed, a previously unexamined variable behind my own "50 more" drift, since that follow-up under-specifies the original prompt's constraint. [Student A]'s DeepSeek-versus-Llama comparison, where DeepSeek "does not fail to produce coherent stories, it just has a different default story," tests something the subject paper only speculates about: that the physical-object bias in Benzon's lists is "an abstraction over the concepts in the universe of texts on which ChatGPT was trained." [Student B] cites a third Benzon paper reporting that bare "story" prompts converge nineteen out of twenty times on the same narrative archetype, the same default attractor, for stories instead of lists.

One of the assigned reference readings turns out to be Benzon's own 1985 report "Ontology in Knowledge Representation," self-cited in the subject paper's own appendix, and it supplies the theoretical skeleton underneath the subject paper's 2023 experiments: closer kin than an outside citation would suggest. That paper's assignment tree, object, plant, animal, human, social person, with properties inherited upward, is, node for node, the Great Chain of Being that the subject paper spends its "From Salt and NaCl to the Great Chain" section probing, and Benzon cross-references this explicitly via a footnote to his own 2018 paper on the assignment relation. The sharpest instance sits inside the 20 Questions transcripts. In the "Apple" round, ChatGPT's route runs through the substantive hierarchy, "Is it a plant?", "Is it a type of tree?", "Is it a deciduous tree?", "Is it a fruit-bearing tree?", while an earlier round detours through the functional hierarchy of food: kitchen, "fruit used in cooking," red-colored fruit guesses, repeating "currant" twice. The two rounds enact, unprompted, the same substantive-versus-functional split the 1985 paper formalizes explicitly. What looks like Benzon discovering new structure in ChatGPT reads more plausibly as his own thirty-eight-year-old categories surfacing in a system never given them directly, which raises the stakes on whether that structure is really in the model, or is being read into it by an author who already had the categories in hand before prompting.

No single presenter states the session's real shared question directly; it only becomes visible when the presentations are read against each other: whether Benzon's small-sample, hand-picked working-paper method can bear the weight of the general claims it makes. Multiple, independent presentations converge on the same diagnosis and the same fix. [Student C]'s rubric-scored replication, an eight-point coherence rubric run across thirty trials on three models, turns Benzon's impressionistic reading into a countable outcome. [Student D] proposes a protocol, generate n stories per condition, control model version, temperature, and session history, test statistically, reframing Benzon's method as needing to move from observation to characterization. Benzon himself half-anticipates this, rerunning one transformation "at the beginning of a session several days after" the original to check reproducibility. My own dictionary-balancing fix is the same diagnose-an-uncontrolled-variable-then-formalize-it move, run on the subject paper itself. Three independent attempts reaching for the same remedy, larger samples, controlled sampling, is stronger evidence of a real, session-wide gap than any one alone.

The subject paper's own headline finding is the clearest casualty of that rigor problem. [Student E], presenting on Benzon's 1985 paper, argues from theory that large language models are fluent on the surface but exhibit "no explicit ontological structure, only statistical patterns," performing unreliably in "high sensitivity domains," a verdict reached without ever seeing my own result. My own controlled rerun, finding no significant concrete/abstract bias once sampling is balanced, is the empirical confirmation of that same verdict, aimed at the specific claim, the abstract/concrete asymmetry, that looked most like real ontological competence. Benzon's own hedge, "What universe are we sampling and how do we choose our samples?", turns out to have been the right question, and it gets answered, in the negative, against his own finding, by a controlled rerun of his own experiment: the kind of check no outside critic supplied. This is the strongest connection the whole session makes to the subject paper: a theory-driven skepticism and a controlled-sampling rerun, arrived at independently, landing on the same conclusion.

So, landscape or artifact? The honest answer is mixed, and asymmetric across the subject paper's two experiments. The "20 things" landscape and its recurring prototype survive stress-testing reasonably well, echoed independently in two other students' story experiments and deepened by the 1985 assignment-tree machinery, which turns up unprompted in the paper's own 20 Questions transcripts. The concrete/abstract asymmetry does not fare as well. It is the paper's most quotable, headline-sounding finding, and it does not survive its own author's method being taken seriously, once my own rerun runs the controlled version his own hedge called for. That asymmetry is, in the end, the paper's most interesting result, precisely because it fails this way. An informal, small-sample, behavioral method can generate metaphors that turn out to be real and durable, while also generating findings that do not survive contact with rigor, and the session as a whole, read together rather than paper by paper, turns out to be a case study in telling those two outcomes apart. Benzon opened the door to that test himself, with a question he never answered: what universe are we sampling, and how do we choose our samples. Someone in his own seminar just had to walk through it.
