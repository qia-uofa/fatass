William Benzon writes about ChatGPT the way one might write about an animal that cannot be dissected. He never looks inside the model. He only submits prompts, records what comes back, and reads a pattern across a handful of tries. From that method he draws claims about structure: that ChatGPT sorts things into ontological categories descended from something like the Great Chain of Being, that its stories about hope settle into a repeatable prototype, that it treats abstract concepts differently from concrete ones. The question this essay asks is whether that structure belongs to the model or to the method that found it. A handful of informal trials, each one a single prompt read once for its pattern, is a thin basis for a general claim about how a language model organizes meaning. Benzon's working paper, "ChatGPT's Ontological Landscape," is the case this essay will use to test that question. Its own sharpest evidence, on either side, turns out to come from tightening one of its own experiments rather than from anything outside it.

Two experiments carry that method's full weight. Asked repeatedly for "a list of 20 things, anything," ChatGPT returns lists that differ item by item but are drawn, Benzon writes, from "roughly the same conceptual territory": physical objects skewed toward a narrow, recurring set — bicycle, hot air balloon, chocolate chip cookies, rainbow. He reads this through Waddington's epigenetic landscape: a prompt drops a "ball" into activation space, and it rolls downhill into a valley shaped by "the lay of the land." In "ChatGPT Plays 20 Questions," six targets, each played twice, split the same way: bicycle, squid, and apple ran 19–31 questions with several hints; justice and evolution resolved in 7–9 with none; truth sat between at 19–21 but needed the most hints of any round. The real wager is as much methodological as substantive: that repeated, informal trials like these, without ever touching weights or activations, can license a real claim about category structure. Benzon hedges it himself, at once, calling it "premature to ascribe any statistical significance to these findings" and asking, "What universe are we sampling and how do we choose our samples?"

This essay's own sidenote runs a deliberate variant of that list prompt, not a repeat of it: give me 20 things, anything, start with butterfly, dragonfly. ChatGPT fills out the "-fly" pattern first, then drifts into flying insects, then insects generally, then flying mammals; pressed for 50 more, it collapses further into mammals of any kind. Where Benzon drops the ball into a broad, shallow valley and watches where it settles, this drops it into a narrow, seeded pocket and traces its escape back toward that valley, testing a mechanism his landscape metaphor implies but never runs, since his own trials are always fresh 20-item prompts, not an in-context extension of one already running. Held to his own standard, though, the sidenote is thin: no model version or session date, against his care to log "the September 25, version of ChatGPT"; one run, against his three reruns of a single list, one after logging out, to check whether it was "rigid"; and no word on temperature, which he names outright as the variable governing how much choice is available at all.

The sidenote's second experiment is where this essay makes its strongest evaluative move. It starts from Benzon's 20-Questions numbers with a hypothesis: ChatGPT's first question is always abstract versus concrete, and because abstract words are rarer in the dictionary, an abstract target takes fewer steps to pin down. Generalized, that becomes a claim about binary questions: a yes/no split should favor guessing the less frequent branch first. Testing it catches a confound: the concrete/abstract split among Benzon's six targets doesn't match the true ratio in the English dictionary, so the asymmetry he reports could be an artifact of which six words he picked, not the model's questioning strategy. The fix samples from a custom dictionary with an even split and tells ChatGPT the target comes from that list; run this way, the bias disappears. A second overclaim falls with it. Benzon's own transcripts don't open on abstract versus concrete at all: justice runs "Is it a living thing? No. Is it an object? No. Is it a concept or idea? Yes," question three, not question one. Both corrections apply the scrutiny his hedge in paragraph 2 called for, but never ran, to his own sampling.

A companion presentation stress-tests the same landscape claim. Replicating Benzon's session-context design on Llama-3.1-8B, the presenter found single-session runs coherent 10 out of 10, separate-session runs 7 out of 10, three collapsing into token salad; their reframe: "session context affects stability, not results." Set beside the sidenote's "50 more" drift, this reads as the same spectrum's far end: a soft slide into a genericized category there, that same slide run to incoherence here. The same presentation isolates a second variable: "story" alone held 7 out of 10 coherent, "tell me a story" and "write a story" each held 10 out of 10, specificity rather than session history apparently buying the stability, a candidate the sidenote's own "50 more" follow-up never tested, since it under-specifies the constraint its first prompt set. A third comparison, DeepSeek against Llama, found DeepSeek converging on a different default story rather than failing to converge: an actual test of what the subject paper only speculates, that such a default is "an abstraction over the concepts in the universe of texts" a model was trained on. A second presentation adds one more point: a third Benzon paper it cites found bare "story" prompts converging on the same archetype nineteen times out of twenty, the same attractor confirmed rather than complicated.

The third reference paper closes this comparison out, and it is the strongest connection of the three because it is not really a comparison at all. Reference analysis confirms that paper3, "Ontology in Knowledge Representation," is Benzon's own 1985 report, self-cited in the subject paper's own Appendix. Its assignment tree — object, plant, animal, human, social person, properties inherited upward — is, node for node, the Great Chain of Being the subject paper spends its "From Salt and NaCl to the Great Chain" section probing in ChatGPT dialogue, and Benzon cross-references this himself, footnoting his own 2018 paper on the assignment relation at the exact point he introduces that hierarchy. The sharpest single instance sits in the 20-Questions "Apple" round. There ChatGPT's route runs through the substantive hierarchy paper3 formalizes: is it a plant, is it a type of tree, is it a deciduous tree, is it a fruit-bearing tree. An earlier round instead detours through the functional hierarchy the same paper distinguishes from it: related to food, kitchen, fruit used in cooking, then a run of red-colored guesses that repeats "currant" twice before landing on apple. ChatGPT was never given paper3 directly, yet it re-enacts the substantive-versus-functional split paper3 states outright. That is not Benzon finding new structure in the model. It is his own thirty-eight-year-old categories turning up again, unprompted, in a system he already had a name for before he started typing.

No single presenter said so, but read against each other, the session kept returning to one question: whether Benzon's method, a handful of hand-picked prompts read once for their pattern, can carry the general claims he draws from them. Three separate efforts land on the same diagnosis and reach for the same remedy. One student's replication scores coherence on an eight-point rubric across thirty trials and three models, turning Benzon's impressionistic reading into a number that can be checked. Another lays out a protocol: generate several stories per condition, hold model version, temperature, and session history fixed, then test the result statistically, reframing Benzon's method as needing to move, in that presenter's words, "from observation to characterisation." Benzon anticipates the complaint himself, inside the same paper, rerunning one transformation "at the beginning of a session several days after" the first to check whether it still held. The sidenote's fix to the concrete/abstract confound is that same move, run a third time on the subject paper itself. Three independent presentations reaching for the same remedy is stronger evidence of a real, session-wide gap than any one of them alone.

Naco's presentation reaches its verdict from theory alone, without ever seeing the sidenote's numbers: LLMs are fluent on the surface but carry "no explicit ontological structure, only statistical patterns," and that gap shows up worst in "high sensitivity domains." The sidenote's rerun supplies the missing empirical half, and it lands on the one place in the subject paper that looked most like real ontological competence: the abstract/concrete split in 20 Questions. Balance the sampling, and the split disappears. Theory and evidence, reached independently, converge on the same claim. The irony is exact. Benzon's own hedge, "What universe are we sampling and how do we choose our samples?," turns out to have named the actual flaw, and it gets answered in the negative, against his own finding, not by an outside critic but by a controlled rerun of his own experiment. That convergence is the strongest connection this session makes to the subject paper.

The question this essay opened with was whether the structure Benzon keeps finding belongs to the model or to the method that found it. His two experiments answer that differently, and unevenly. The 20 things phenomenon holds up. The companion presentations found the same pull toward a narrow set of defaults on other models, and the third reference paper traced it back to Benzon's own 1985 assignment tree, still doing the same work thirty-eight years on. Independent replication and the model's own author's older work both point the same direction. The 20-Questions asymmetry does not hold up. It is the paper's most quotable result, and it is the one that disappears once its own sampling is balanced the way Benzon's hedge asked for. That split is itself the paper's most interesting result, more telling than either finding alone, and it cuts against the paper's own apparent conclusion for that reason: an informal method, a handful of prompts read once each, can produce one finding that survives stress-testing from three directions and one that collapses once its own author's standard is finally taken seriously. Read across the whole session rather than paper by paper, that is the actual work here: one case of a loose method succeeding, one case of it failing.
