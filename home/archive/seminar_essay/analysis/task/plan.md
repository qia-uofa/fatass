# Plan

## 1. Task analysis

Source read: `nodes/examples/writing/seminar/materials/task/writting_assignment.md`
(the only non-empty file in the task node's readable directory).

**Deliverable.** A short analytical text, 1200–1800 words, written by the
student *after* their presentation.

**Required content (three explicit criteria).**
1. Discuss the core argument and contribution of the paper the student
   presented (the subject paper).
2. Analyze how that paper relates to the other papers presented in the
   same seminar session (the reference papers).
3. Reflect on broader themes, unresolved questions, or tensions across
   the presented works as a set.

**Explicit constraint.** The task twice warns against summary: it asks
for "critical thinking and synthesis," and states outright — "we are
looking for discussion of the general subject of the symposium **not**
a summary of the paper you presented." This is the sharpest constraint
in the brief and the one most likely to be missed by a writer who
defaults to paper-report mode.

**Form.** Explicitly unconstrained — "no strict form that we expect
your essay to adhere to." Three blog posts are offered as optional
inspiration (Intro to LLMs; Unsupervised Models of the Brain;
Generalized Visual Language Models), signaling an essayistic,
opinionated register is acceptable and arguably preferred over a
formal academic structure with numbered sections.

**Extras (informal addenda, still binding).**
- Incorporate the student's own presentation into the essay.
- Give particular weight to the target/subject paper.

**No stated grading rubric, citation format, or source-count
requirement** — the task does not say how many reference papers must
be engaged, whether outside sources are allowed, or how it will be
scored beyond the three bullet criteria above.

**Ambiguities / easy misreadings.**
- Criterion 1 ("discuss the core argument and contribution") sits in
  tension with the anti-summary warning. A literal reading of
  criterion 1 alone would produce exactly the summary the task
  disclaims. The resolution implied by the rest of the text is that
  the *argument* should be discussed critically/evaluatively (its
  stakes, its move, what it costs or buys), not narrated
  section-by-section.
- "General subject of the symposium" is undefined — it must be
  inferred from what the presented papers collectively share, not
  taken as a given topic label.
- "The other papers presented in the same seminar session" implies a
  fixed, known set of reference papers; this node's readable
  directory (the task description only) does not contain that set —
  see Open Questions.
- "Incorporate the student's presentation" could mean citing its
  framing/slides, or simply writing the essay from the position the
  presentation already staked out. The task doesn't specify which.

## 2. Proposed essay outline

Target length 1500 words (mid-range of 1200–1800); percentages below
are share of that budget. Per this node's own governing instructions,
the subject paper + our sidenote on it must anchor the largest,
most central sections; reference-paper material is supporting/
contrasting evidence in service of that anchor, not co-equal content.

| # | Working title | Weight | Covers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Framing: the symposium's real question | ~10% (150w) | Opens on the *general subject* of the session (per the task's explicit instruction), stated as a question or tension the whole essay will answer — not a paper introduction. The subject paper is named here only as the essay's anchor/lens, not yet argued. |
| 2 | The subject paper's wager (+ our sidenote) | ~32% (480w) | THE anchor section. States the subject paper's core argument and contribution (criterion 1) evaluatively, not descriptively — what it claims, what it stakes, what would have to be true. Our own sidenote does the real work here: it supplies the critical angle, extension, or objection that keeps this from reading as summary. This section should read as *our* argument about the paper, built with the sidenote, not a report of the paper's contents. |
| 3 | Held up against the room | ~25% (375w) | Criterion 2. Brings in reference papers, but each one enters only to sharpen what section 2 already claimed — as convergent evidence, a foil, or a limit case. Every reference-derived point should be traceable to "this shows something about the subject paper's argument," not treated as a parallel mini-summary of another paper. |
| 4 | The unresolved question | ~20% (300w) | Criterion 3. Names the tension/open question that section 3's comparisons surfaced. Still organized around the subject paper's stance — asks what the field (as sampled by the session) still owes an answer to, given what the subject paper claims. |
| 5 | Where this leaves us | ~13% (195w) | Closes on the general-subject framing from section 1, now answered/complicated rather than restated. Light touch — no new material. |

**Sections where the task's own criteria call for emphasis other than
the subject paper — flagged per instructions:**
- **Section 3** is explicitly required by criterion 2 to be about
  *other* papers' relation to the subject paper. It cannot be
  subject-paper-only content. It stays subordinate by construction —
  every comparison is written as "what this tells us about the
  subject paper's move" rather than "what this other paper argues" —
  but the task genuinely requires reference-paper content to appear
  here, at real (25%) weight.
- **Section 4** is explicitly required by criterion 3 to synthesize
  *across* all presented works, and the task's anti-summary warning
  ("general subject of the symposium") most directly targets this
  section. Its content is the session's shared tension, not the
  subject paper alone — but it is still framed through the position
  the subject paper stakes out, per this node's governing instruction,
  rather than as a neutral survey of the session.
- No section should be pure subject-paper exposition (criterion-1-only
  content) — even section 2, the anchor, is required by the task to
  avoid summary, which is why the sidenote (not the paper alone) does
  the load-bearing work there.

## 3. Open questions / risks before drafting

- **This node has no access to the subject paper, our sidenote, or
  the reference papers/sidenotes** — only the task description was
  readable here. Section 2's actual argument, and which
  reference-paper points earn a place in sections 3–4, can't be
  fixed until that content is read (presumably in the sibling
  `analysis.subject` / `analysis.references` nodes).
- The identity and count of "the other papers presented in the same
  seminar session" is unknown from this file alone — confirm against
  `materials/papers/references/` before drafting section 3, since the
  outline's 25% weight assumes there's more than one comparison point
  to work with.
- Resolve the criterion-1 vs. anti-summary tension explicitly before
  writing section 2: decide the one or two evaluative claims the
  sidenote will make about the subject paper, so that section doesn't
  drift into paraphrase under word pressure.
- Confirm whether "incorporate the student's presentation" (extras)
  means citing/quoting specific framing from the presentation itself,
  or just writing consistently with the position it took — affects
  whether a presentation artifact needs to be read as a source too.
- No rubric or scoring weights are given, so the weighting above is
  inferred from criteria emphasis and the governing subject-paper-first
  instruction, not from the task text itself — worth a sanity check
  once actual source material is in hand, in case the subject paper
  turns out to be thin and reference papers necessarily need more
  room.
- Form is unconstrained (no headers required) — decide before
  drafting whether to keep the five working-title sections as visible
  headers or fold them into continuous essay prose, per the
  blog-post-style inspiration the task offers.
