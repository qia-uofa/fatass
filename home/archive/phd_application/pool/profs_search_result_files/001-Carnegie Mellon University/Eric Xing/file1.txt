Andrew Carnegie – Wikipedia Article

Andrew Carnegie (1835–1919) was a Scottish-born American industrialist and philanthropist who became one of the wealthiest people of his era through steel manufacturing.

Early Life and Career

Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland, to a weaver father. Due to mechanization reducing hand-weaving opportunities, his family emigrated to Pennsylvania in 1848. At age 13, he worked as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill, earning $1.20 weekly while attending evening classes.

By age 14, Carnegie had moved into telegraph work and caught the attention of Thomas A. Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad, who made him his secretary. During the Civil War, Carnegie organized military telegraph systems in Washington, D.C.

Steel Industry Success

After the war, Carnegie systematically invested in iron foundries and steel works. Impressed by Henry Bessemer's steel conversion technology during British visits, he established his first blast furnace in 1870. His Edgar Thomson Works (1873) became highly profitable after securing contracts from his former employer.

Through strategic partnerships and consolidation, particularly his 1881 merger with Henry Clay Frick's coke operations, Carnegie built the world's largest steel company by 1892. He sold his enterprise to J.P. Morgan in 1901 for $480 million, enabling his retirement.

Philanthropic Legacy

Carnegie donated over $350 million (approximately $11.9 billion today) to various causes:

- Libraries: Funded 2,509 public libraries worldwide, with 1,681 in the United States
- Carnegie Hall: Financed this iconic New York concert venue
- International Peace: Established the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace in 1910
- Education: Founded Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University)
- Scientific Research: Created the Carnegie Institution of Washington

He articulated his philosophy in his 1889 essay The Gospel of Wealth, stating: "The man who dies rich dies in disgrace," emphasizing that the wealthy should distribute their fortunes to benefit society.

Legacy and Criticism

While celebrated for philanthropic achievements, Carnegie faced significant criticism for labor practices. Workers in his steel mills endured grueling conditions, low wages, and dangerous environments. The 1892 Homestead Strike—during which ten people died—particularly tarnished his reputation, as he bore responsibility despite being in Scotland at the time.

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts. His wife, Louise Whitfield Carnegie, survived him by 26 years.
