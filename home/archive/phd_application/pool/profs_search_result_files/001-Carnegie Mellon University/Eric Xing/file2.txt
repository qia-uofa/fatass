# Andrew Carnegie: Life and Legacy

## Early Life and Background

Andrew Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland, to William Carnegie and Margaret Morrison Carnegie. His father was a successful weaver who owned multiple looms. The family lived modestly in a shared weaver's cottage before moving to a larger home in 1836.

Carnegie's maternal uncle, George Lauder Sr., significantly influenced his childhood by introducing him to Scottish literature and historical figures. His cousin George Lauder later became his business partner in America.

When Carnegie was 12, his father's weaving business declined due to industrial changes. His mother became the primary breadwinner by operating a small shop and assisting her brother. Facing economic hardship, the family borrowed money and emigrated to the United States in 1848, settling in Allegheny, Pennsylvania.

## Early Career

Carnegie's first job in September 1848 was as a bobbin boy at Anchor Cotton Mills, earning $1.20 weekly for 12-hour workdays. He quickly advanced to other positions, eventually earning $2.00 per week at John Hay's bobbin factory.

In 1849, Carnegie became a telegraph messenger boy, where he "memorized all of the locations of Pittsburgh's businesses" and learned to translate signals by ear. Colonel James Anderson's private library of 400 volumes, open to working boys on Saturday nights, profoundly influenced Carnegie's intellectual development. This experience motivated his later commitment to establishing public libraries.

## Railroad Years

Starting in 1853, Thomas A. Scott of the Pennsylvania Railroad employed Carnegie as a secretary and telegraph operator at $4.00 weekly. Scott became a mentor, teaching him management and cost control. Carnegie advanced to superintendent of the Western Division in 1859 at age 24, earning $1,500 annually.

Scott facilitated Carnegie's early investments, including a $500 investment in the Adams Express Company in 1855 (secured by his mother's mortgage on their home). Through railroad connections, Carnegie invested in sleeping car companies and other railroad-related enterprises.

## Civil War Period

In spring 1861, Carnegie joined the U.S. Military Telegraph Corps under Scott, who was now Assistant Secretary of War. As Superintendent of Military Railways and Union telegraph lines in the East, Carnegie helped open rail lines to Washington, D.C., and supervised troop transportation after the Battle of Bull Run.

## Transition to Steel

After the Civil War, Carnegie left railroads for ironworks. He formed the Keystone Bridge Works and Union Ironworks in Pittsburgh, leveraging his connections with Pennsylvania Railroad executives to secure contracts.

Through Keystone, Carnegie supplied steel for the Eads Bridge across the Mississippi River (completed 1874), an important demonstration project for steel technology.

## Steel Empire (1875-1900)

Carnegie made his fortune through two major innovations: adopting the Bessemer process for cheap, efficient steel production, and vertically integrating all raw material suppliers. He acquired the Homestead Steel Works in 1883, which included coal and iron fields, railways, and lake steamships.

By 1889, Carnegie Steel was the world's largest manufacturer of pig iron, steel rails, and coke, producing approximately 2,000 tons of pig iron daily. He combined various operations under the Carnegie Steel Company in 1892.

Carnegie's success also depended on favorable tariffs, which he actively lobbied Congress to maintain. His business practices included market manipulation and pricing coordination with railroad barons rather than allowing free-market competition.

## Sale to U.S. Steel

In 1901, at age 65, Carnegie sold his enterprises to J.P. Morgan for $303,450,000. Morgan, seeking to create an integrated steel industry, organized the United States Steel Corporation—the first corporation with over $1 billion market capitalization.

Carnegie received $225.64 million in 5% gold bonds, making him wealthier than John D. Rockefeller at the time. He then devoted himself entirely to philanthropy.

## Philanthropic Vision

In 1868, at age 33, Carnegie wrote about his intentions to limit his income and "spend the surplus each year for benevolent purposes." He envisioned settling in Oxford or London to pursue education and public service rather than accumulating wealth.

His 1889 article "The Gospel of Wealth" argued that wealthy individuals should distribute their fortunes to benefit society. The piece supported progressive taxation and estate taxes, inspiring widespread philanthropic activity.

## Libraries and Public Institutions

Carnegie's most prominent philanthropic achievement was establishing approximately 3,000 public libraries across the United States, Britain, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand. The first opened in 1883 in Dunfermline.

His library model required local authorities to provide land and operating budgets while Carnegie funded construction and equipment. Between 1885 and his later years, he donated substantial sums: $500,000 to Pittsburgh, $250,000 to Allegheny City, and £50,000 to Edinburgh.

However, historians note that Carnegie's libraries also served as "institutions of control." According to scholar Richard White, mill workers "despised" his Homestead library because "Carnegie imposed work rules that deprived his employees of virtually all their leisure." Library boards dominated by mill officers controlled reading materials to "direct the reader to the desired information."

## Education and Scientific Research

Carnegie invested heavily in education:
- $2 million in 1900 to establish the Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University)
- $2 million in 1902 for the Carnegie Institution in Washington, D.C.
- $10 million in 1901 to create the Carnegie Trust for Scottish Universities
- Service on boards of Cornell University and Stevens Institute of Technology

He donated an additional $10 million to the Carnegie Institution in 1911 to expedite construction of the Hooker Telescope at Mount Wilson Observatory. The telescope achieved first light in November 1917, before his death.

## Music, Peace, and Other Causes

Carnegie funded the construction of 7,000 pipe organs in churches and temples and built Carnegie Hall in New York City (opened 1891). He was a founding financial backer of the National Conservatory of Music of America.

His peace advocacy led to contributing $1.5 million in 1903 for The Hague's Peace Palace. He founded the Carnegie Hero Fund in 1904 and eventually established it across multiple countries to recognize heroic deeds.

He established pension funds for former Homestead employees (1901) and college professors (1905), the latter evolving into TIAA-CREF.

He supported Black American education through substantial benefactions to Tuskegee Institute under Booker T. Washington and helped create the National Negro Business League.

## Anti-Imperialism

Following the Spanish-American War, Carnegie strongly opposed American imperial expansion. He opposed Philippine annexation and personally offered $20 million to help Filipinos purchase independence from the United States. In 1898, he joined the American Anti-Imperialist League alongside former presidents Grover Cleveland and Benjamin Harrison.

## Controversies

### Johnstown Flood (1889)

Carnegie was one of 50-plus members of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club, blamed for the Johnstown Flood disaster that killed 2,209 people. The club's poorly maintained dam, located 20 miles upstream from Johnstown, Pennsylvania, failed on May 31, 1889, after heavy snowmelt and spring rains.

Club members, including Carnegie and Henry Clay Frick, formed the Pittsburgh Relief Committee following the disaster but agreed "never to speak publicly about the club or the flood." Despite potential legal liability, attorneys successfully prevented lawsuits against club members.

Carnegie later built Johnstown a new library to replace one destroyed in the flood.

### Homestead Strike (1892)

The Homestead Strike lasted 143 days in 1892, centered on Carnegie Steel's main plant. The conflict involved the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers and the Carnegie Steel Company over wages and working conditions.

Carnegie departed for Scotland before the crisis peaked, leaving Henry Clay Frick, an ardent anti-union manager, to handle negotiations. When the union and company failed to reach agreement, Frick locked out workers and hired thousands of strikebreakers and 300 Pinkerton agents.

On July 6, fighting erupted between Pinkertons and strikers, killing ten men (seven strikers, three Pinkertons) and injuring hundreds. Pennsylvania Governor Robert Pattison deployed state militia. An anarchist later attempted to assassinate Frick in response to the violence.

## Personal Life

Carnegie married Louise Whitfield in 1887. They had one daughter, Margaret Carnegie Miller. He maintained residences in Scotland, New York, and Massachusetts.

He cultivated friendships with notable figures including poet Matthew Arnold, philosopher Herbert Spencer, and humorist Mark Twain. He corresponded with U.S. Presidents and was influenced by Herbert Spencer's evolutionary thought and laissez-faire economics.

## Death and Legacy

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts, from bronchial pneumonia. He had already distributed approximately $350.7 million (roughly $6.9 billion in 2025 dollars)—nearly 90 percent of his fortune—to charities, foundations, and universities. After his death, his remaining $30 million went to foundations and pensioners.

He was buried at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in Sleepy Hollow, New York, beneath an intentionally simple Celtic cross gravestone, located near labor organizer Samuel Gompers.

His legacy includes hundreds of institutions bearing his name: Carnegie Hall, Carnegie Mellon University, the Carnegie Institution for Science, Carnegie Corporation of New York, Carnegie Endowment for International Peace, and numerous libraries, museums, and educational organizations worldwide.
