# Andrew Carnegie: Scottish-American Industrial Magnate and Philanthropist

## Overview

Andrew Carnegie (1835-1919) was a Scottish-American industrialist who became one of the wealthiest figures in history through his steel empire, then devoted his later years to extensive philanthropy. Born in Dunfermline, Scotland, he immigrated to Pennsylvania at age 12 and built a fortune that he largely redistributed to public causes.

## Early Career and Rise to Wealth

Carnegie began as a cotton mill worker earning $1.20 weekly before advancing to telegraph operator. Working for the Pennsylvania Railroad, he learned business management while accumulating capital through strategic investments in railroads, sleeping cars, and bridges.

During the Civil War, Carnegie served with the U.S. Military Telegraph Corps, helping organize rail and telegraph lines for the Union cause. After the war, he transitioned to iron and steel production, establishing the Keystone Bridge Company and later developing an integrated steel operation.

His crucial innovation involved adopting the Bessemer process for mass-producing steel cheaply and efficiently. Combined with vertical integration of raw materials and suppliers, Carnegie built an unprecedented industrial empire. By the late 1880s, his Carnegie Steel Company was the world's largest producer of pig iron, steel rails, and coke.

## The 1901 Sale and Retirement

In 1901, J.P. Morgan purchased Carnegie's steel enterprises for $303.45 million—making Carnegie even wealthier than John D. Rockefeller. This transaction formed the foundation of U.S. Steel Corporation. Carnegie then retired from business, having accumulated roughly $225.64 million personally.

## Philanthropic Legacy

Carnegie devoted his final eighteen years to giving away approximately $350 million (nearly 90% of his fortune). His 1889 article "The Gospel of Wealth" articulated his philosophy that wealthy individuals should distribute their fortunes to benefit society.

**Major contributions included:**

- **Libraries**: Funded approximately 3,000 public libraries across the United States, Britain, Canada, and other English-speaking nations
- **Education**: Established Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University), Carnegie Institution for Science, and Carnegie Trust for Scottish Universities
- **Cultural institutions**: Built Carnegie Hall in New York City and funded 7,000 pipe organs for churches
- **Peace initiatives**: Donated $1.5 million toward the Peace Palace in The Hague
- **Specialized funds**: Created the Carnegie Hero Fund, pension programs for employees and college professors, and the Carnegie Corporation of New York

## Controversies

**Johnstown Flood (1889)**: Carnegie was among members of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club, which operated a poorly maintained dam that catastrophically failed, killing 2,209 people. Club members successfully avoided legal liability through coordinated legal defense.

**Homestead Strike (1892)**: A violent labor confrontation erupted at Carnegie Steel's Homestead plant when management, led by Henry Clay Frick, demanded wage cuts affecting hundreds of workers. The arrival of 300 Pinkerton agents resulted in deaths and injuries. Carnegie was absent in Scotland during the crisis.

## Philosophy and Influence

Carnegie advocated for progressive taxation and estate taxes while promoting laissez-faire economics. He was influenced by Herbert Spencer's evolutionary thought and befriended major intellectual figures including Matthew Arnold, Mark Twain, and William Ewart Gladstone.

He opposed American imperialism, offering $20 million to help the Philippines purchase independence from the United States and joining the American Anti-Imperialist League in 1898.

## Death and Legacy

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, from bronchial pneumonia at his Massachusetts estate. He was buried at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in New York, near labor leader Samuel Gompers. His institutions continue influencing education, libraries, scientific research, and international peace efforts worldwide.
