Andrew Carnegie - Main Article Content

Andrew Carnegie (1835-1919) was a Scottish-born American industrialist and philanthropist who became one of the wealthiest individuals of his era through the steel industry.

Early Life and Career

Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland, to a weaver father. His family emigrated to Pennsylvania in 1848 as mechanization made hand-weaving obsolete. At age 13, he began working in a cotton mill, earning $1.20 weekly while attending evening classes.

By age 14, Carnegie worked at a telegraph office, where his abilities caught the attention of Pennsylvania Railroad official Thomas A. Scott, who became his mentor and employer.

Steel Industry Success

After the Civil War, Carnegie left railroads to build his own enterprises. Recognizing the potential of Henry Bessemer's steel-making innovations, he constructed his first blast furnace in 1870. In 1873, he established a steel mill named after J. Edgar Thompson, a Pennsylvania Railroad president, which secured major contracts from that company.

By 1892, Carnegie's consolidated operations formed the Carnegie Steel Company—then the world's largest steel manufacturer. In 1901, he sold his company to banker J.P. Morgan for $480 million, who merged it into U.S. Steel.

Philanthropic Legacy

Carnegie believed "the man who dies rich dies in disgrace." He donated over $350 million (approximately $11.9 billion in modern value) to various causes:

- Libraries: Financed 2,509 public libraries globally, with 1,681 in America
- Carnegie Hall: Funded this renowned New York concert venue
- Education: Founded Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University) and Carnegie Institution of Washington
- Peace: Established the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace with $10 million
- Cultural initiatives: Sponsored the Carnegie International art exhibition and Pittencrieff Park in his hometown

Criticism

Despite philanthropic achievements, Carnegie faced criticism for labor practices. According to biographical accounts, he paid workers meager wages while working them seven days weekly. The 1892 Homestead Strike—where Carnegie's business partner hired strikebreakers resulting in deaths—became a controversial moment in his legacy, though he accepted personal responsibility without publicly condemning his partner's actions.

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts.
