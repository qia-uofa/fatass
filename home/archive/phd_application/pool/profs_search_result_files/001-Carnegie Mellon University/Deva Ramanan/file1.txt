Andrew Carnegie - Main Content

Early Life & Background

Andrew Carnegie was born November 25, 1835, in Dunfermline, Scotland, to weaver William Carnegie and Margaret Morrison. His family emigrated to the United States in 1848, settling in Allegheny, Pennsylvania.

Career Development

Carnegie began working at age 13 as a cotton mill bobbin boy earning $1.20 weekly. At 14, he moved to telegraph work in Pittsburgh, where he caught the attention of Pennsylvania Railroad official Thomas A. Scott, eventually becoming his secretary. During the Civil War, he assisted Scott in organizing military telegraph systems in Washington, D.C.

Steel Industry Success

After the war, Carnegie transitioned from railroads to steel. He recognized the potential of Henry Bessemer's steel production methods during visits to Britain. In 1870, he built his first blast furnace; by 1873, he established a major steel mill. Through strategic partnerships—including a crucial 1881 merger with coke producer Henry Clay Frick—Carnegie built the world's largest steel company by the 1890s.

In 1901, Carnegie sold his steel operations to J.P. Morgan for $480 million, making him extraordinarily wealthy.

Philanthropic Legacy

Carnegie donated over $350 million (approximately $11.9 billion today) to various causes:

- Libraries: Funded 2,509 public libraries across America and Europe
- Carnegie Hall: Financed this iconic New York concert venue
- Peace Organizations: Established the Carnegie Endowment for International Peace with $10 million
- Education: Founded Carnegie Institute of Technology (now Carnegie Mellon University)
- Scientific Research: Created the Carnegie Institution of Washington
- Hero Funds: Established recognition programs for selfless individuals

His 1889 essay "The Gospel of Wealth" articulated his belief that wealthy individuals should distribute their fortunes for public benefit.

Criticism

Despite philanthropic achievements, Carnegie faced scrutiny for labor practices. His steel mills generated enormous profits while workers faced dangerous conditions, low wages, and long hours. The 1892 Homestead Strike, during which his partner Frick hired strike-breakers resulting in deaths and injuries, particularly damaged his reputation.

Death & Legacy

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts. He was survived by his wife Louise Whitfield Carnegie and their daughter Margaret. His name remains attached to numerous institutions, libraries, and even a dinosaur species (Diplodocus carnegii).
