Andrew Carnegie

Andrew Carnegie (November 25, 1835 – August 11, 1919) was a Scottish-American industrialist and philanthropist who led the expansion of the American steel industry in the late 19th century and became one of history's richest Americans.

Early Life

Carnegie was born in Dunfermline, Scotland, to William Carnegie and Margaret Morrison Carnegie in a modest weaver's cottage. His father owned multiple looms and the family eventually moved to a larger house on Edgar Street. Carnegie received education at the Free School in Dunfermline.

His maternal uncle, Scottish political leader George Lauder Sr., profoundly influenced young Carnegie by introducing him to the works of Robert Burns and Scottish historical figures like Robert the Bruce and William Wallace. Lauder's son, also named George, became Carnegie's childhood companion and later his business partner.

When Carnegie was twelve, his father struggled as a handloom weaver during difficult economic times. His mother became the primary breadwinner through various efforts. The family immigrated to Allegheny, Pennsylvania in 1848, seeking better opportunities.

Early Career

Carnegie's first job in America was as a bobbin boy in a cotton mill, earning $1.20 per week. He then worked for bobbin manufacturer John Hay at $2.00 weekly. In 1849, he became a telegraph messenger boy and quickly learned to translate signals by ear without paper slips.

Colonel James Anderson opened his personal library of 400 volumes to working boys, inspiring Carnegie's later commitment to public libraries. Carnegie worked for the Pennsylvania Railroad starting in 1853 under Thomas A. Scott, eventually becoming superintendent of the Western Division at age twenty-four.

Business Investments and Growth

Through Pennsylvania Railroad connections, Carnegie made early investments including $500 in the Adams Express Company (financed by his mother's mortgage on their home) and shares in Theodore Tuttle Woodruff's sleeping car company. Before the Civil War, he arranged a merger between Woodruff's company and George Pullman's enterprise, profiting significantly.

During the American Civil War, Carnegie served in the U.S. Military Telegraph Corps under Scott, who was now Assistant Secretary of War. He supervised transportation of defeated Union forces after Bull Run and helped open rail lines into Washington, D.C.

Steel Industry

After the Civil War, Carnegie shifted focus to ironworks, eventually forming Keystone Bridge Works and Union Ironworks in Pittsburgh. Through these ventures, he supplied steel for the landmark Eads Bridge across the Mississippi River at St. Louis (completed 1874), an important proof-of-concept for steel technology.

In 1868, at age thirty-three, Carnegie declared intentions to limit his income to $50,000 annually and dedicate surplus wealth to benevolent purposes, though he continued building his empire for several more decades.

Steel Empire Expansion (1875-1900)

Carnegie's fortune derived from two major innovations: adopting and adapting the Bessemer process for cheap, efficient mass steel production, and implementing vertical integration of raw material suppliers. In 1883, he acquired Homestead Steel Works, which included coal and iron fields, a 425-mile railway, and lake steamships.

By 1889, U.S. steel output exceeded Britain's, with Carnegie controlling a substantial portion. His enterprises included the J. Edgar Thomson Steel Works in Braddock, Pittsburgh Bessemer Steel Works, Lucy Furnaces, Union Iron Mills, Keystone Bridge Works, Hartman Steel Works, Frick Coke Company, and Scotia ore mines. These were consolidated under Carnegie Steel Company in 1892.

His success relied partly on railroad industry relationships, which negotiated prices rather than competing freely in markets. Carnegie also benefited from U.S. trade tariffs, spending considerable resources lobbying Congress for favorable rates, earning millions annually through these protections.

Sale to J.P. Morgan (1901)

At age sixty-five, Carnegie considered retirement and reorganized enterprises as conventional joint-stock corporations. Banker J.P. Morgan, recognizing Carnegie's efficiency, envisioned an integrated steel industry eliminating duplication and waste. After negotiations concluded March 2, 1901, Morgan formed United States Steel Corporation—the first corporation with over $1 billion market capitalization.

Carnegie's steel enterprises sold for $303,450,000. His personal share amounted to approximately $225.64 million (valued at $8.73 billion in 2025 dollars), paid as 5%, 50-year gold bonds.

Scholar and Activist (1880-1900)

While managing his businesses, Carnegie contributed regularly to magazines including The Nineteenth Century and North American Review. He befriended English poet Matthew Arnold, philosopher Herbert Spencer, and humorist Mark Twain, maintaining correspondence with most U.S. presidents, statesmen, and notable writers.

In 1879, he funded swimming-baths for Dunfermline residents. The following year, he provided £8,000 for a Dunfermline Carnegie Library. In 1884, he donated $50,000 to Bellevue Hospital Medical College for a histological laboratory (now called Carnegie Laboratory).

A 1881 family trip to Britain highlighted his mother laying the foundation stone for a Carnegie-funded library in Dunfermline. Carnegie partnered with Samuel Storey in the early 1880s to purchase British newspapers advocating monarchy abolition and establishment of "the British Republic." His charm and wealth cultivated friendships with prominent Britons, including Prime Minister William Ewart Gladstone.

In 1886, his younger brother Thomas died at age forty-three. That same year, Carnegie published Triumphant Democracy, arguing American republican government was superior to British monarchy. The book, featuring an upended crown and broken scepter on its cover, sparked considerable UK controversy yet sold over 40,000 copies, mostly in America.

In 1889, Carnegie published "Wealth" in North American Review. After Gladstone's request, it appeared in Britain as "The Gospel of Wealth" in The Pall Mall Gazette. Carnegie argued wealthy industrialists should divide their lives into two phases: accumulating wealth, then distributing it to benevolent causes, making philanthropy essential to meaningful existence.

Anti-Imperialism

Following the Spanish-American War, as America considered annexing Cuba, Guam, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines, Carnegie strongly opposed American colonies. He nearly supported William Jennings Bryan against McKinley in 1900 over Philippine annexation.

In 1898, Carnegie attempted arranging Philippine independence. When the U.S. purchased the Philippines from Spain for $20 million, Carnegie personally offered $20 million to enable Filipinos to purchase independence from America—an offer that yielded nothing.

That year, Carnegie joined the American Anti-Imperialist League, opposing U.S. Philippine annexation alongside former presidents Grover Cleveland and Benjamin Harrison and literary figures including Mark Twain.

Philanthropist (1901-1919)

From 1901 forward, public attention shifted from Carnegie's business acumen to his philanthropic dedication. During the last eighteen years of his life, he gave approximately $350 million (roughly $6.9 billion in 2025 dollars)—nearly ninety percent of his fortune—to charities, foundations, and universities.

Public Libraries

Among his most prominent efforts was establishing approximately 3,000 public libraries in forty-seven U.S. states and numerous other locations including Canada, Britain, Ireland, Belgium, Serbia, France, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the West Indies, and Fiji. His method provided construction and equipment funds conditionally upon local authorities supplying land and operational budgets.

The first Carnegie Library opened in 1883 in Dunfermline. In 1885, he gave $500,000 to Pittsburgh for a public library; in 1886, $250,000 to Allegheny City for a music hall and library; and $250,000 to Edinburgh for a free library. He also donated £50,000 helping establish the University of Birmingham in 1899.

Between 1886 and 1917, Carnegie reformed both library philanthropy and design, encouraging closer correspondence between architectural efficiency and philanthropic intentions. However, historian Richard White notes that steelworkers often "despised" Carnegie's Homestead library, as Carnegie imposed work rules eliminating leisure time, then lectured workers on using time they didn't possess. Mill officers dominated library boards, and professional staff asserted "the library has the right to control the character of the reading."

Education and Science

In 1900, Carnegie gave $2 million founding the Carnegie Institute of Technology (CIT) at Pittsburgh and $2 million in 1902 creating the Carnegie Institution in Washington, D.C., to encourage research and discovery. CIT is now Carnegie Mellon University following merger with Mellon Institute of Industrial Research. Carnegie also served on boards at Cornell University and Stevens Institute of Technology.

In 1911, Carnegie became a sympathetic benefactor to astronomer George Ellery Hale's efforts building the 100-inch Hooker Telescope at Mount Wilson Observatory in California, donating an additional ten million dollars to the Carnegie Institution with hopes the work would "repay to the old land some part of the debt we owe them by revealing more clearly than ever to them the new heavens." The telescope achieved first light November 2, 1917, with Carnegie still living.

In 1901, Carnegie gave $10 million establishing the Carnegie Trust for the Universities of Scotland through a deed signed June 7, 1901, and incorporated by royal charter August 21, 1902. This unprecedented sum exceeded total annual government assistance to all four Scottish universities (approximately £50,000). The Trust aimed improving scientific research opportunities and enabling qualified Scottish youth to attend university.

Carnegie was elected Lord Rector of University of St. Andrews in December 1901, formally installed in October 1902, serving until 1907. He donated substantial sums to Dunfermline beyond the library, purchasing the private Pittencrieff estate which he opened to all public members, establishing the Carnegie Dunfermline Trust benefiting his hometown. A statue commemorating his park creation was built 1913-1914.

Music and the Arts

Carnegie was a major music patron and founding financial backer of Jeannette Thurber's National Conservatory of Music of America in 1885. He built Carnegie Hall in New York City (opened 1891), remaining in his family until 1925. His music interests led him funding construction of 7,000 pipe organs in churches and temples, with no apparent denominational preferences.

In 1913, Carnegie gave $10 million endowing the Carnegie United Kingdom Trust, a grant-making foundation. He transferred charge of existing and future benefactions (except UK university benefactions) to trustees, who instituted policies financing rural library schemes rather than erecting buildings and assisting public musical education rather than granting church organs.

Additional Philanthropic Work

Carnegie provided $25,000 annually to the spelling reform movement, creating the Simplified Spelling Board which produced the Handbook of Simplified Spelling, written entirely in reformed spelling.

In 1901, Carnegie established large pension funds for former Homestead employees and, in 1905, for American college professors. The latter evolved into TIAA-CREF. One requirement stipulated that church-related schools sever religious connections to receive his funding.

Carnegie was a substantial benefactor of Tuskegee Institute for Black American education under Booker T. Washington, helping Washington establish the National Negro Business League.

In 1904, he founded the Carnegie Hero Fund for the United States and Canada (later established in the United Kingdom, Switzerland, Norway, Sweden, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Denmark, and Germany) recognizing heroic deeds. He contributed $1.5 million in 1903 for the Peace Palace at The Hague and $150,000 for a Pan-American Palace in Washington as a home for the International Bureau of American Republics.

Unable to distribute his entire fortune during his lifetime, Carnegie established the Carnegie Corporation of New York in 1911 "to promote the advancement and diffusion of knowledge and understanding" and continue his giving program.

Carnegie was honored for philanthropy and arts support through honorary membership initiation in Phi Mu Alpha Sinfonia fraternity October 14, 1917, at the New England Conservatory of Music in Boston, Massachusetts. The fraternity's mission reflects Carnegie's values by developing young men sharing talents creating world harmony.

Death

Carnegie died August 11, 1919, in Lenox, Massachusetts, at his Shadow Brook estate, of bronchial pneumonia. He had given away $350,695,653 (approximately $6.9 billion in 2025 dollars). Following his death, remaining $30 million went to foundations, charities, and pensioners.

He was buried at Sleepy Hollow Cemetery in Sleepy Hollow, New York, with an intentionally simple Celtic cross gravestone at the Arcadia Hebron plot corner of Summit Avenue and Dingle Road. Carnegie rests only yards from union organizer Samuel Gompers, another important Gilded Age industry figure.

Controversies

Johnstown Flood (1889)

Carnegie was one of more than fifty members of the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club blamed for the Johnstown Flood killing 2,209 people in 1889.

Partner Henry Clay Frick formed this exclusive club at Frick's suggestion above Johnstown, Pennsylvania, comprising leading Western Pennsylvania business tycoons including Andrew Mellon, attorneys Philander Knox and James Hay Reed, and Frick himself. Near South Fork township, the South Fork Dam (built 1838-1853 by Pennsylvania Commonwealth for canal system reservoir use) was abandoned when railroads superseded canal transport, subsequently sold to Pennsylvania Railroad, then to private interests, eventually owned by the South Fork Fishing and Hunting Club in 1881.

The dam was seventy-two feet high and 931 feet long. Between 1881 club opening and 1889, it frequently sprang leaks patched with mud and straw. A previous owner removed and sold three cast-iron discharge pipes for scrap that previously allowed controlled water release. Cambria Iron Works downstream raised integrity concerns. Repair work, height reduction, and unusually high snowmelt combined with heavy spring rains caused the dam to fail May 31, 1889, releasing twenty million tons of water in the Johnstown Flood.

When Pittsburgh received telegraph notification of dam failure, Frick and other club members formed the Pittsburgh Relief Committee assisting flood victims while determining never publicly discussing the club or flood. This strategy succeeded, with Knox and Reed fending off all lawsuits blaming club members.

Though Cambria Iron and Steel's facilities suffered heavy flood damage, they returned to full production within one year. Afterward, Carnegie built Johnstown a new library replacing one destroyed in the flood, built by Cambria's chief legal counsel Cyrus Elder. The Carnegie-donated library is now owned by the Johnstown Area Heritage Association and houses the Flood Museum.

Homestead Strike (1892)

The Homestead Strike was a bloody 143-day labor confrontation in 1892, among the most serious in U.S. history, centered on Carnegie Steel's primary Homestead, Pennsylvania plant, growing from labor dispute between the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers (AA) and Carnegie Steel Company.

Carnegie traveled to Scotland before unrest peaked, leaving mediation to his anti-union associate and partner Henry Clay Frick. With the collective bargaining agreement expiring June-end, Frick and local AA leaders negotiated beginning in February. With steel industry prosperity and higher prices, the AA requested wage increase (representing about 800 of 3,800 plant workers). Frick countered with average 22% wage decrease affecting nearly half union membership and removing positions from the bargaining unit.

Union and company failed reaching agreement, and management locked out workers. Workers considered this management "lockout" rather than worker "strike," meaning they possessed rights to protest, with subsequent government action being criminal procedures designed crushing the pivotal growing labor rights movement, strongly opposed by management. Frick brought thousands of strikebreakers to work mills and Pinkerton agents safeguarding them.

July 6, arrival of 300 Pinkerton agents from New York City and Chicago resulted in fighting killing ten men (seven strikers, three Pinkertons) and injuring hundreds. Pennsylvania Governor Robert Pattison ordered two state militia brigades to the site.
