To know a large language model without opening it up, then, is not to possess a stable map of what it "contains," but to hold a description that is only as trustworthy as the last cheap experiment that tried to break it. The session's real contribution is not any one landscape, prototype, or hierarchy but the demonstration, replayed three times over, that behavioral probing carries its own falsification machinery built in — which is exactly what lets a student sidenote outgrow the paper it annotates. For the field, this suggests the vocabulary of "emergent structure" should be treated less as a finding and more as a standing hypothesis, one that behavioral work can propose but cannot alone confirm. Settling how much of that vocabulary survives contact with the underlying corpus and prompt statistics will likely require pairing this outside-in probing with some outside-in check of its own — controlled corpus ablations, or a modest bridge to mechanistic evidence — so that the next landscape claimed is one that has already met its own rigor, rather than waiting for someone else's sidenote to supply it.
