Benzon's "ChatGPT's Ontological Landscape" argues that large language models do not merely produce fluent text but implicitly organize the concepts behind that text into a structured, non-random "landscape" of categories — one that echoes long-standing philosophical distinctions such as the Aristotelian Great Chain of Being and the split between concrete, common-sense concepts (salt) and abstract, scientific ones (NaCl). Benzon's method is exploratory and dialogic rather than statistical: he interrogates ChatGPT through a series of targeted prompts and games, first asking it to reason about canonical philosophical cases (salt/NaCl, Morning Star/Evening Star, the Great Chain, Aristotle's tripartite soul), then probing its default associations with open prompts like "give me 20 things," and finally using the parlor game 20 Questions — played twice each against six targets ranging from concrete (bicycle, apple) to abstract (justice, truth) — as a structured diagnostic of how efficiently the model navigates its own conceptual space. The recurring finding, that ChatGPT tends to need fewer questions to converge on abstract targets than concrete ones, is offered less as a statistical result (Benzon is explicit that six targets is far too small a sample) than as evidence that the model's internal organization of concepts is not arbitrary but shaped by regularities in its training distribution. The paper's contribution, then, is less a specific empirical claim than a demonstration of method: it shows that classic philosophical apparatus for describing conceptual hierarchies can be repurposed as a lightweight, interpretable probe for a black-box system, giving humanists a way to ask what a language model knows about the structure of concepts without requiring access to its internals.
