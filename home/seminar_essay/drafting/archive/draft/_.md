What does it mean to know a large language model, when the one tool least available to us is a look inside its weights? This essay is not a review of any single talk from the session; it is an attempt to answer that question as the session as a whole posed it. Across the papers under discussion, a shared commitment emerges: rather than treating a model like ChatGPT as an opaque autocomplete engine or waiting on mechanistic interpretability to "pop the hood," each author instead probes the system from the outside, reading its organization off the regularities in what it says under controlled prompting. But the papers arrive at this stance from different starting points and press it toward different ends, and no single one states the underlying method as a method. It is only by setting them beside each other that the shape of a shared research program comes into view.

William L. Benzon's "ChatGPT's Ontological Landscape" argues that large language models do not merely produce fluent text but implicitly organize the concepts behind that text into a structured, non-random "landscape" of categories — one that echoes long-standing philosophical distinctions such as the Aristotelian Great Chain of Being and the split between concrete, common-sense concepts (salt) and abstract, scientific ones (NaCl). Benzon's method is exploratory and dialogic rather than statistical: he interrogates ChatGPT through a series of targeted prompts and games, first asking it to reason about canonical philosophical cases (salt/NaCl, Morning Star/Evening Star, the Great Chain, Aristotle's tripartite soul), then probing its default associations with open prompts like "give me 20 things," and finally using the parlor game 20 Questions — played twice each against six targets ranging from concrete (bicycle, apple) to abstract (justice, truth) — as a structured diagnostic of how efficiently the model navigates its own conceptual space. The recurring finding, that ChatGPT tends to need fewer questions to converge on abstract targets than concrete ones, is offered less as a statistical result (Benzon is explicit that six targets is far too small a sample) than as evidence that the model's internal organization of concepts is not arbitrary but shaped by regularities in its training distribution. The paper's contribution, then, is less a specific empirical claim than a demonstration of method: it shows that classic philosophical apparatus for describing conceptual hierarchies can be repurposed as a lightweight, interpretable probe for a black-box system, giving humanists a way to ask what a language model knows about the structure of concepts without requiring access to its internals.

Benzon's approach rests on a set of framing choices worth making explicit before comparing it to the other work engaged with in this session. Methodologically, the paper treats ChatGPT's outputs as symptomatic rather than authoritative: a list that drifts from "butterfly, dragonfly" toward generic flying bugs, or a 20 Questions transcript, is read not as evidence about the world but as a trace of how the model has organized its internal conceptual space. This licenses an interpretive move borrowed wholesale from human cognitive science — Aristotle's Great Chain of Being, Waddington's epigenetic landscape, the intension/extension distinction behind Morning Star and Evening Star — imported as ready-made vocabulary for describing a system whose actual computational substrate looks nothing like a nervous system or a developmental process. The paper is explicit, too, about what it is not attempting: with only two trials per target in the 20 Questions game, it disclaims any statistical claim and offers its findings as suggestive probes rather than confirmed results. That combination — analogical borrowing from human ontology on one side, self-conscious methodological modesty on the other — stakes out a particular kind of problem-space: exploratory mapping of an LLM's latent categorical structure through behavioral probing, rather than either mechanistic interpretation of model internals or rigorous quantitative benchmarking. Left aside, correspondingly, is any attempt to ground these ontological categories in the model's training data, architecture, or attention patterns. Naming these commitments now gives the comparison that follows a shared vocabulary — exploratory/behavioral versus mechanistic, small-sample/suggestive versus statistically validated — for locating where the reference papers agree, diverge, or push further than Benzon was willing to go.

Benzon's "Ontological Landscape" sits closely beside his own companion piece, "ChatGPT Tells 20 Versions of Its Prototypical Story," and the pairing is instructive precisely because the two papers share a method while asking different questions of it. Both rely on mass-sampling repeated ChatGPT outputs under minimal prompting — one collects twenty stories from the bare word "story," the other collects lists and 20-Questions transcripts — and both treat the resulting variation, not any single output, as the real data: recurring patterns (a fairy-tale protagonist named "Elara," a drift from "xxx-fly" words to general mammals) are read as traces of a stable underlying structure rather than noise. Where they diverge is in what kind of structure they claim to find. The prototypical-story paper argues for a single dominant attractor — one narrative shape the model reliably falls into — and treats variation across trials (single-session versus separate-session) as evidence about how tightly that attractor is fixed. "Ontological Landscape," by contrast, is explicitly interested in gradation and hierarchy: not one fixed point but a landscape of categories at different levels of abstraction, with the 20 Questions results suggesting the model navigates that landscape unevenly depending on vocabulary frequency. This contrast reveals two competing pictures of what black-box probing can uncover in a generative model: a discrete "default" (best captured by prototype-and-deviation analysis) versus a continuous, structured space (best captured by category and salience). Both are compatible with Benzon's broader methodological commitment — that studying inputs and outputs, not internals, can still license claims about a model's latent organization — but they stake out different corners of the same design space for what "structure" in an LLM's behavior can mean.

The session's other two papers extend the comparison along a different axis: not shared method, but differing appetite for theoretical explanation. Benzon's "ChatGPT Tells Stories, and a Note about Reverse Engineering" shares his authorship and his behavioral-probing method, but it does not stop at describing a pattern — it proposes a mechanism, conjecturing that story generation is governed by a nested hierarchy of probability distributions running from whole trajectory down to individual token, and it stratifies description itself into surface, middle, and bottom levels borrowed from cognitive science and Anthropic's mechanistic-interpretability work. "Ontological Landscape," by contrast, stays almost entirely at the surface level: it reports that ChatGPT's category judgments are uneven and metaphorizes this as a "landscape," but declines to say what, mechanistically, produces the unevenness. Benzon's 1985 paper, "Ontology in Knowledge Representation," pushes the same divide further still by supplying an explicit, hand-built theory — the paradigmatic-versus-assignment tree distinction — for exactly the kind of category behavior the subject paper only observes empirically in ChatGPT. Read together, the three papers trace an arc from formal theory (1985, explicit trees, no learning involved) through conjectured mechanism (the reverse-engineering paper, a hypothesized but unverified hierarchy inside a black box) to pure behavioral description (the subject paper, which catalogs the landscape's shape without committing to why it has that shape). This raises a question the subject paper leaves open: whether its "landscape" metaphor is a placeholder for a structure like Benzon's earlier ontological trees, or evidence that ChatGPT's categories are only ever statistical approximations that resist that kind of explicit formalization altogether.

Read together, these three papers and their student sidenotes converge on a single method — treat the model as a black box, hammer it with repeated or perturbed prompts, and read the resulting patterns as a trace of internal structure — but they also expose a tension that no individual paper confronts head-on: is the "structure" this method uncovers a genuine property of what the model has learned, or an artifact of how the experiment was set up? Benzon's own papers are unabashedly anecdotal, drawing sweeping claims (a stable ontological landscape, a prototypical fairy-tale story, a hierarchy of nested probability distributions) from a handful of trials, and he defends this looseness explicitly, framing behavioral probing as a legitimate alternative to mechanistic interpretability rather than a placeholder for it. The student sidenotes, however, keep independently pulling in the opposite direction, toward rigor the source papers never asked for: the Ontological Landscape sidenote discovers that the "abstract targets are easier to guess" finding evaporates once the concrete/abstract word ratio is controlled for; the prototypical-story sidenote builds an eight-point coherence rubric and finds that swapping model families (Llama versus DeepSeek) changes the prototype itself; the reverse-engineering sidenote proposes embedding-based distance metrics and statistical testing where Benzon offered only paragraph-by-paragraph impression. In each case, the act of tightening the experiment doesn't just refine Benzon's claim — it partially dissolves it, relocating the apparent structure from "the model's ontology" or "the model's narrative instinct" to something more mundane: word frequency in the prompt, or idiosyncrasies of a particular training corpus. What emerges from the juxtaposition, then, is not a confirmation of Benzon's method but a quiet audit of it in real time — a live demonstration that anecdotal reverse-engineering of LLMs is generative precisely because it is falsifiable by cheap, small-scale follow-up, even though (or because) its original claims were never built to survive that scrutiny. This leaves an open question the papers collectively raise but none answers: how much of the "emergent structure" language currently used to describe LLM behavior — landscapes, prototypes, hierarchies — actually names something intrinsic to the model, as opposed to being a compact description of biases in the corpus and prompts used to elicit it.

To know a large language model without opening it up, then, is not to possess a stable map of what it "contains," but to hold a description that is only as trustworthy as the last cheap experiment that tried to break it. The session's real contribution is not any one landscape, prototype, or hierarchy but the demonstration, replayed three times over, that behavioral probing carries its own falsification machinery built in — which is exactly what lets a student sidenote outgrow the paper it annotates. For the field, this suggests the vocabulary of "emergent structure" should be treated less as a finding and more as a standing hypothesis, one that behavioral work can propose but cannot alone confirm. Settling how much of that vocabulary survives contact with the underlying corpus and prompt statistics will likely require pairing this outside-in probing with some outside-in check of its own — controlled corpus ablations, or a modest bridge to mechanistic evidence — so that the next landscape claimed is one that has already met its own rigor, rather than waiting for someone else's sidenote to supply it.
