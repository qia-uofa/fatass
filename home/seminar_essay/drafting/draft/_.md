This session's seminar kept circling back to one question: when a large language model like ChatGPT is stripped of explicit structure and simply asked to produce something — a list, an answer, a story — what does the shape of what comes back reveal about how it organizes knowledge internally? I presented William Benzon's "ChatGPT's Ontological Landscape," which probes that question through deceptively simple games, open-ended list generation and twenty-questions-style guessing, to see how the model carves the world into concrete and abstract categories. But the paper is only one entry point into a conversation the session was already having with itself. Read alongside the other papers presented that day, on ChatGPT's prototypical story generation, on reverse-engineering its narrative behavior, and on formal ontology in knowledge representation, Benzon's probing stops looking like an isolated experiment and starts looking like one instance of a shared methodological bet: that a model's black-box outputs, examined carefully enough, expose the structure underneath. This essay treats that shared bet, and the tensions it produces, as its real subject, rather than retreading the presented paper on its own.

Benzon's central claim is that you can learn something real about how a large language model organizes knowledge without ever opening it up, simply by handing it tasks whose surface simplicity forces an implicit ontology to show itself. Asked to list "things in a kitchen" or to play twenty questions toward a hidden concept, ChatGPT does not recite a stored taxonomy; it improvises one, and the improvisation is patterned rather than random, favoring certain groupings of concrete objects over others and drifting predictably between abstract and concrete framings depending on how a prompt is phrased. The paper's contribution is less a specific taxonomy of what ChatGPT "knows" than a demonstration of method: behavioral probing, treated as a legitimate way to infer structure in a system whose internals are otherwise opaque, whether for architectural reasons or simply scale. That matters beyond this one model. As language models get folded into search, tutoring, and knowledge work, understanding the shape of their implicit ontologies, what they cluster together, what they treat as basic-level versus superordinate, bears directly on where they'll be reliable and where they'll quietly impose a categorization scheme nobody asked for. Benzon's games are small, almost playful, but they stake out a claim I want to hold onto for the rest of this essay: that a model's outputs, pressed at the right seams, are themselves a kind of evidence.

My own presentation leaned into that claim by turning it into something closer to a live experiment than a report on Benzon's results. Rather than walking through his twenty-questions trials, I reran the game myself, seeding ChatGPT with "give me 20 things, anything" and watching it drift from a narrow lexical pattern into broader superordinate categories once that pattern exhausted itself, a small, live extension of Benzon's landscape metaphor rather than a summary of it. The emphasis I chose, though, was complicating rather than confirming his headline result. Benzon found ChatGPT guesses abstract targets faster than concrete ones in twenty questions; I proposed a mechanistic reason, that a forced either/or question favors whichever option is rarer in English, then noticed my own test's word ratio didn't match the dictionary's real distribution. Rerunning the game with a balanced list erased the asymmetry entirely. I presented that null result not as a refutation of Benzon but as a caution: the landscape his method reveals may be inseparable from the vocabulary used to probe it.

That caution about vocabulary dependence turns out to sit inside a larger pattern the session kept producing, one I only recognized fully once I set my paper next to Ann-Kathrin Harl's presentation on ChatGPT's prototypical story generation. Her paper asks the model for nothing more than the single word "story," twenty times over, ten in one session and ten in separate ones, and treats the recurring fairy-tale shape that comes back, a named protagonist like "Elara," an exile, a return, as evidence of a stable narrative attractor sitting underneath the model's outputs. That is structurally the same move Benzon makes with list generation and twenty questions, and the same move I made rerunning both: don't ask what the model "means," ask what it keeps doing under repetition, and read the repetition itself as data. Harl's paper functions less as a separate case than as a companion instance of that bet, narrative sequence in her case, category structure in Benzon's and mine, and the convergence is reassuring in one sense: both of us found genuine bias under sustained querying, hers in name variety shifting with session history, mine in categories drifting from a narrow lexical seed toward broader superordinate ones. But the convergence is also exactly why my null result should worry her too. If my abstract/concrete asymmetry dissolved once I balanced the probing vocabulary, her fairy-tale prototype invites the same question: how much of its shape reflects ChatGPT's training corpus, and how much reflects the specific, unexamined statistics of the word "story" itself as a prompt.

Dilekcan Pamir's presentation, on a companion Benzon paper reverse-engineering ChatGPT's story generation through character-substitution experiments, sharpens that worry into an explicit methodological demand rather than a discovery I stumbled into. Pamir translates Benzon's narrative vocabulary, story trajectory, segment, coherence, into computer-science terms and positions the black-box probing beside the Haralick-Ramesh performance-characterization framework from vision science: perturb a system, measure what survives, and use that survival pattern to characterize behavior without opening the system up. That is recognizably the same reverse-engineering stance Benzon brings to my own categorical probing and Harl's narrative probing, applied to a third facet, story structure rather than category structure or plot recurrence. But where I found the vocabulary-dependence problem almost by accident, rerunning a test until an asymmetry vanished, Pamir names it as a limitation up front: Benzon's experiments are qualitative, single-example, uncontrolled for session history or model version, and Pamir proposes fixing this with multiple trials per condition, embedding distance metrics, and statistical testing, the same controlled resampling that would have caught my own artifact before I stumbled onto it. Pamir's own extension, running synthetic Markov-sequence experiments on a Transformer under bit-flip noise, tests whether output stability reflects learned structure rather than narrative convention, mirroring my instinct to build a small parallel experiment rather than take Benzon's result on faith. Read together, the two extensions suggest the session's shared method has a shared blind spot: black-box probing keeps finding real patterns without stress-testing whether they'd survive a differently designed probe.

Stepping back from the individual comparisons, the session's real subject was never any single model behavior but a question about method: can you trust structure you only ever see through outputs, never through the mechanism that produced them? Benzon's category drift, Harl's recurring "Elara," and Pamir's perturbation trials are three attempts to answer yes, each treating a different surface, categorical, narrative, structural, as a window onto something stable underneath. But the session's fourth paper, on formal ontology in knowledge representation, answers a version of that question from the opposite direction entirely, and setting it against the other three exposes the tension I only skirted earlier. Formal ontology treats structure as something built and verified by construction, an engineer specifies relations, checks consistency, and the ontology simply is what was declared. Behavioral probing offers no such guarantee: my own vanishing asymmetry showed that what looks like stable category structure can be an artifact of the specific words used to ask. That is a disagreement about mechanism, not just method, whether the "ontology" a language model exhibits is a genuine internal structure being sampled, or a locally coherent illusion generated fresh each time in response to whatever probe it is handed, with no persistent fact of the matter underneath. The three probing papers largely finesse this by treating patterned repetition as good enough evidence on its own; the formal ontology paper implicitly rejects that standard, since nothing short of an explicit, checkable specification would count as an ontology in its terms. Neither side directly refutes the other in this session's papers, they are simply not answering the same question, but the juxtaposition suggests that "ontology" is doing incompatible work across the room, and no one presenting fully reckoned with that incompatibility out loud.

Two questions follow from that unreckoned incompatibility, and none of the session's papers fully answers either. First: is there any output-level evidence that could actually settle whether a model's apparent ontology is a persistent structure or a fresh illusion assembled per-probe, or does behavioral probing simply lack the resolution to distinguish the two in principle? My own vanishing asymmetry suggests the latter, but a single null result cannot carry that conclusion alone. Second, and more pointed for my own paper's Benzon lineage: his 1985 ontology paper is read, in the fourth presentation, as posing whether LLMs solve his old inheritance problem or merely sidestep it fluently, and that verdict is asserted rather than tested against anything ChatGPT actually does. Addressing either question would mean pairing Pamir's proposed statistical rigor, repeated trials, controlled vocabulary, embedding-distance measures, with genuine mechanistic evidence: attention patterns, induction heads, something below the output surface, rather than reading repetition itself as proof of structure. Until probing and interpretability meet, "ontology" will keep meaning something different, and something less settled, every time this room uses the word.

Read back against the session as a whole, Benzon's category-drift experiments look less like a self-contained result about ChatGPT's kitchens and hidden concepts, and more like the opening move in a shared, unresolved bet: that patterned repetition under probing counts as evidence of real structure. My own rerun sharpened that bet into a warning, Harl's fairy-tale attractor and Pamir's perturbation trials extended it, and the formal ontology paper exposed how much the whole room was assuming without saying so. None of us settled whether what we found was a stable ontology or a probe-shaped illusion, and none of us paired behavioral evidence with anything mechanistic enough to decide the question. What the synthesis suggests, then, is that "probing reveals structure" remains a working hypothesis for this research area, not a finding, one that needs controlled vocabulary and interpretability evidence before the next presenter can claim more than I did.
