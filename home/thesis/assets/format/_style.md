# Style Guide — "An Ontological Perspective on LLM Beliefs"

This is a companion to the format guide: format covers what's mandated about the document's physical/administrative shape, this covers how it should actually read. It's built from the writer's profile (their working style, priorities, and the pushback they've already accepted) and from standard conventions for empirical CS/ML bachelor's theses — the two are meant to combine, not compete: fit the field's norms, but let the writer's actual reasoning voice come through in the parts of the thesis that are argument rather than method-reporting.

## Voice and register

- **Formal academic prose, but not inflated.** Standard scientific register for CS/ML writing: precise, declarative, third-person or passive for methodology ("the model was probed at answer-adjacent positions" / "we probe..."), first-person plural ("we") is fine and common in ML papers if the department doesn't object — avoid singular "I" in Methods/Results, but singular "I" is appropriate and often better in Discussion/Limitations where you're making a judgment call, not reporting a procedure.
- **Don't let chat-register terseness leak in.** The writer's own messages are short, lowercase, sometimes missing articles — that's a fast interactive register, not a writing style, and shouldn't be mistaken for the target register of the thesis itself. The thesis prose should be complete, grammatical, formally punctuated sentences even where the writer's own thinking-out-loud was compressed.
- **Hedge in proportion to evidence, not more, not less.** This is the single most load-bearing stylistic instruction from the writer's own priorities: the realistic conclusion is something like "the pattern is more consistent with an information-caching/retrieval architecture than a naive introspective self-report framing" — not a resolved metaphysical claim. Every strong-sounding claim in Results/Discussion should be checked against this: does the evidence actually support this exact strength of statement, or is it one notch stronger than what was shown? When in doubt, weaken the verb ("suggests" / "is consistent with" rather than "shows" / "proves") rather than the finding itself.

## Directness about limitations — carry the writer's own instinct through

The writer explicitly asked for critical pushback during planning and responded well to a blunt assessment ("pure replication is thin," "don't overpromise the ontological payoff") rather than needing it softened. That same directness should show up in the thesis's own Discussion/Limitations chapter, in the writer's own authorial voice:

- State plainly where the extension's evidence is thin, correlational, or underpowered, rather than burying it in hedged academic-passive constructions that technically disclose but functionally obscure ("it is possible that further work may be needed to fully elucidate..." is the pattern to avoid; "the sample size here is too small to distinguish these two readings" is the pattern to aim for).
- This is not license for informality — it's a instruction about *content*, not register: be formally precise *and* say the honest, sometimes unflattering thing about your own results.
- Mirror the writer's willingness (shown already in their own prior small experiments, `papers/presentation.md`) to report a null or partially-falsified result as a real finding rather than something to minimize. If the epistemic-emotion extension only partially discriminates between ontological readings, say that directly in the abstract and conclusion, not just in a buried limitations paragraph.

## Terminology

- **Define borrowed philosophical vocabulary on first use, and keep it visibly distinct from the ML vocabulary.** Terms like "ontological transition" and "paradigmatic transition" come from Benzon's informal essays, not from a peer-reviewed or standardized ML/philosophy source — the first time each appears, define it explicitly and cite Benzon, and don't let it silently drift into being used as if it were established field terminology.
- **Be explicit about the evidentiary asymmetry between your two anchors.** Kumaran et al. 2026 is a peer-reviewed empirical ICML paper; Benzon's work is descriptive/interpretive essay writing, not itself empirically validated. The thesis should say this difference out loud somewhere in Related Work or Background (e.g., "Benzon's ontological framework is used here as a conceptual lens for interpreting the mechanistic findings, not as an independently validated empirical claim") rather than let the two sources read as equally weighted evidence.
- **Keep "confidence" precisely scoped.** Given how much conversational effort went into pinning down exactly why confidence has an independent ground-truth signal (TriviaQA gold answers) and what would be needed for other epistemic states to have the same, the write-up should carry that same precision: every time "confidence" or an epistemic emotion is invoked, be clear about whether it's the model's verbalized self-report, an internally probed signal, or a ground-truth-anchored quantity — these are different objects and the thesis's core argument depends on not conflating them.
- **Name-check the title shift once, briefly, and move on.** Since the working title ("...LLM Confidence") and the official title ("...LLM Beliefs") differ, it's worth one sentence early on (e.g., in the introduction or a footnote) clarifying that "belief" is being used in the sense the official title intends, and how it relates to the narrower technical construct "confidence" the empirical chapters actually measure — otherwise a reader may wonder why a thesis titled about "beliefs" spends most of its empirical weight on "confidence."

## Sentence and paragraph conventions

- **Topic-sentence-first paragraphs.** Especially in Methodology/Results — state the claim or step first, then the supporting detail. This is standard for readability in technical writing and makes the thesis skimmable for examiners who will not read linearly.
- **One claim per paragraph** in Discussion — resist stacking multiple interpretive claims in one block; each deserves its own paragraph so a reader (and the writer, revising) can independently assess each one's evidentiary support.
- **Spell out acronyms on first use** (LLM, ICML, TriviaQA, etc.), even ones that feel obvious after months immersed in the topic — examiners include a second reader (Zweitgutachter/in) who may not share the same background.
- **Avoid rhetorical questions in formal chapters.** The writer uses them naturally in exploratory chat ("is it easy to change 'confidence' into other sentiments...") — that's a good drafting/thinking tool, but in the actual thesis prose, convert rhetorical questions into the explicit research question they're standing in for (put those in the Introduction as numbered RQs) rather than scattering them through the prose.

## AI-disclosure section — style specifically

Given the priority the writer placed on this being honest and not boilerplate: write it in plain, specific, first-person voice (this is one place first-person singular "I" fits naturally, since it's an authorial statement about process, not a scientific claim) — name the tools, name what they were used for concretely (e.g. "code scaffolding for the probing pipeline was AI-assisted via the `fatass`/Claude tooling; research questions, interpretation of results, and the philosophical argument are my own"), and don't pad it with generic disclaimers that could apply to any thesis. A reader should be able to tell from this section specifically what in *this* document was and wasn't AI-touched.

## Language choice

Whichever language is ultimately confirmed (see the format guide's flag on this) — German or English — hold the same formal register throughout; don't mix registers by defaulting to more casual phrasing when writing feels effortful. If the thesis ends up in English (which the officially English-language issued title suggests, per the format guide's inference), write formal academic English, not translated-in-your-head German syntax — e.g. prefer direct English scientific phrasing over calques from the German scientific register ("im Folgenden wird gezeigt" → "the following section shows," not "in the following, it will be shown").
